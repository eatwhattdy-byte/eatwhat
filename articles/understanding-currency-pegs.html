Understanding Currency Pegs

Most of the currency pairs on our converter float freely, meaning their value moves constantly based on market supply and demand. But a meaningful number of currencies don't work that way at all — their value is fixed, or "pegged," to another currency by government policy. Understanding pegs explains why some currencies barely move against the dollar for years, then suddenly move a lot in one day.

What a peg actually is

A currency peg is a commitment by a country's government or central bank to keep its currency's value fixed at (or very close to) a specific exchange rate against another currency, most commonly the US dollar. Instead of letting supply and demand set the price freely, the central bank actively buys and sells its own currency to hold that rate steady.

Why countries choose to peg

Pegging trades away flexibility for predictability. The main reasons a country adopts one:

  • Trade stability. A fixed rate removes exchange rate risk for businesses trading heavily with the currency it's pegged to, which can encourage investment and simplify pricing.
  • Inflation control. Pegging to a currency with a strong track record of low inflation can import some of that credibility and discourage runaway domestic inflation.
  • Confidence. In economies with a history of currency instability, a peg can reassure businesses and foreign investors that the currency's value won't suddenly collapse.

The cost of maintaining one

Holding a peg isn't free. The central bank needs a large reserve of the currency it's pegging to (usually US dollars), which it draws down to buy its own currency whenever market pressure pushes the rate away from the target. If that pressure is sustained and reserves run low, defending the peg becomes harder and harder — this is exactly the situation that precedes most currency crises.

What a peg gives up: a pegged currency can't use its own interest rate policy to respond to local economic conditions the way a floating currency can, since interest rates have to stay in line with defending the peg rather than managing domestic inflation or growth. Economists call this a core trade-off of fixed exchange rate regimes.

What happens when a peg breaks

When a central bank can no longer defend a peg — usually because it's run out of reserves or decided the cost is too high — it either lets the currency float freely (a "de-pegging") or resets the peg at a new, usually weaker, rate (a "devaluation"). Because pegs tend to hold steady right up until they don't, a break can produce a large, sudden move rather than the gradual drift you'd see in a floating currency.

Why this matters for a conversion

If you're regularly converting to or from a pegged currency, the practical takeaway is different from a floating one: day-to-day movement will likely be minimal or zero, but it's worth knowing whether the peg is considered stable or under pressure, since that's where the real risk sits. A currency that's been pegged at the same rate for decades (some Gulf state currencies against the US dollar, for example) behaves very differently from one where a peg is a recent, contested policy.
