The Best Time to Exchange Currency for a Trip

Most travelers lose more money to where and when they exchange currency than to the exchange rate itself moving. The rate on any given day might shift your budget by half a percent either way. The choice between an airport kiosk and a no-fee travel card can shift it by ten percent or more. Here's how to think about the decision.

Airport kiosks: convenient, expensive

Airport currency exchange counters almost always offer the worst rates you'll find, often with a spread of 8–12% below the market rate, plus a flat fee on top. They're pricing in captive demand — you need cash and you need it now. Use them only for a small amount to cover the first taxi or coffee, never for the bulk of a trip's budget.

Banks and credit unions: better, but plan ahead

Ordering foreign currency through your home bank typically gets you a rate much closer to the market mid-rate, sometimes with no fee if you're an existing customer. The catch is timing — many banks need three to five business days to order less common currencies, so this only works if you're planning ahead of departure.

No-foreign-transaction-fee cards: usually the best default

For most trips, a credit or debit card that waives foreign transaction fees will beat both of the above, because card networks generally convert at close to the wholesale market rate. The main things to check before you travel: whether your card charges a foreign transaction fee (many now don't), whether it works reliably in your destination, and whether you have a backup card in case one is declined.

ATMs abroad: good rate, watch the fees

Withdrawing local currency from an ATM at your destination usually gets you a rate close to the market rate, but two fees can eat into that: your home bank's foreign ATM fee, and the local ATM operator's fee. A card that reimburses foreign ATM fees removes most of this downside, and is often the cheapest way to get cash in hand.

A simple rule of thumb: use a fee-free card for the bulk of spending, withdraw cash from ATMs (not kiosks) for what you need in hand, and only use an airport counter for a small emergency amount. Check the live converter a few days before you leave so you know roughly what to expect — not to time the market, just to sanity-check any rate you're offered.

One more thing: "0% commission" isn't 0% cost

Currency exchange counters that advertise no commission are usually building their margin directly into the exchange rate instead of charging it as a separate fee. The way to check is simple: compare the rate they're offering against the live mid-market rate. A gap of more than 2–3% is effectively a hidden fee, no matter what the sign in the window says.
