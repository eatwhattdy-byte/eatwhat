How Central Banks Actually Set Interest Rates

Every time a central bank announces an interest rate decision, currency markets move — sometimes sharply, within seconds. This guide covers what a central bank is actually deciding, how it makes that decision, and why currency traders treat these announcements as some of the most important dates on the calendar.

What rate is actually being set?

When people talk about "the interest rate" a central bank sets, they usually mean a specific benchmark rate — the rate at which commercial banks borrow from or lend to each other overnight, or borrow directly from the central bank. This one rate doesn't directly touch consumers, but it ripples outward: it influences the rates banks charge on mortgages, business loans, savings accounts, and credit cards throughout the economy.

The basic goal: balancing growth and inflation

Most central banks operate with some version of the same mandate: keep inflation low and stable, and support employment and economic growth. These two goals often pull in opposite directions, which is why rate decisions are genuinely difficult calls rather than a mechanical formula.

  • Raising rates makes borrowing more expensive, which tends to slow spending and investment, cooling an overheating economy and reining in inflation — at the cost of slower growth.
  • Cutting rates makes borrowing cheaper, encouraging spending and investment to support growth and employment — at the risk of pushing inflation higher.

How the decision gets made

Major central banks make these decisions through a committee that meets on a fixed schedule (often every six to eight weeks) to review incoming economic data — inflation figures, employment numbers, growth indicators, and forward-looking measures like business and consumer sentiment. The committee votes on whether to raise, cut, or hold the rate, and typically publishes a statement explaining the reasoning, which markets scrutinize as closely as the decision itself.

Why the statement matters as much as the number: markets often react more to the language a central bank uses about future plans than to the rate decision itself, since currency values reflect expectations about where rates are headed, not just where they are today. A rate cut paired with hints of more cuts to come typically weighs on a currency more than the cut alone would.

Why this moves currencies specifically

Higher interest rates make a currency more attractive to hold, because money parked in that currency earns more return. When a central bank raises rates (or signals it will), investors tend to shift capital toward that currency to capture the higher yield, increasing demand and typically strengthening it. A rate cut works in reverse. This is the mechanism behind the "interest rates set the baseline" point in our guide on What Actually Moves an Exchange Rate?

What to actually watch, if you're paying attention to this

For most people converting currency occasionally, tracking every rate decision isn't necessary. But if you're converting a large amount around a known rate announcement date, or you work with a currency pair regularly, it's worth knowing the decision dates for the relevant central banks — a scheduled announcement is one of the more predictable sources of short-term rate volatility, unlike most of the day-to-day noise covered in our guide on exchange rate fundamentals.
