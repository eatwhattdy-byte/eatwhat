Remittance Fees: What You're Really Paying

"No fees" is one of the most misleading phrases in international money transfer. Most providers make their money whether or not they charge a visible fee, because there's a second, quieter place to build in a margin: the exchange rate itself.

The two places a fee can hide

Every transfer provider has two levers to pull. The first is a transparent, upfront fee — a flat charge or a percentage, shown clearly at checkout. The second is the exchange rate they apply, which they can set slightly worse than the mid-market rate without calling it a fee at all. A provider advertising "zero fees" is very often making up the difference entirely through the second lever.

How to find the real cost

The only reliable way to compare providers is to check the exchange rate they're offering against the live mid-market rate at the same moment, then add back any stated fee. A provider quoting a rate 3% below market with no fee is more expensive than one quoting the market rate with a 1% fee — even though the first one calls itself free.

Our converter shows the mid-market rate with no markup, so it's a reasonable benchmark to hold any quote up against before you send money.

What typically drives the margin higher

  • Less common currency pairs. Providers widen their margin on routes with lower trading volume, since they carry more risk holding that currency.
  • Cash pickup options. Sending money for cash collection rather than bank deposit usually costs more, reflecting the provider's own cost of maintaining a cash network.
  • Speed. Instant transfers often carry a higher margin than transfers that settle in one to three business days.
  • Card funding. Paying with a credit card instead of a bank transfer frequently adds an extra percentage, on top of any margin in the rate.

A basic checklist before you send

  1. Check the mid-market rate for your currency pair right before you compare quotes.
  2. Get a quote from at least two providers for the same amount, same payout method.
  3. Calculate the effective cost: (mid-market rate − quoted rate) × amount, plus any stated fee.
  4. Factor in how long the transfer takes if timing matters to the recipient.
Rule of thumb: for a well-known currency corridor (USD–EUR, USD–GBP, USD–INR, and similar), a total effective cost above 2–3% of the transfer amount is on the high side. It's usually worth comparing at least one bank transfer and one dedicated remittance provider before sending a meaningful amount.
