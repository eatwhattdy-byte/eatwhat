Purchasing Power Parity, Explained Simply

Convert your salary into another country's currency at the market exchange rate, and you'll often get a misleading sense of how far that money actually goes there. Purchasing power parity (PPP) is the concept economists use to close that gap — and it's worth understanding even if you never need to calculate it yourself.

The core idea

Purchasing power parity asks a different question than a currency converter does. Instead of "how much foreign currency do I get for my money," it asks "how much can that money actually buy" in each place. If a basket of everyday goods costs $100 in one country and the equivalent basket costs 8,000 units of another country's currency, PPP says the "real" exchange rate — in terms of what money can buy — is 80 units per dollar, regardless of what the market exchange rate happens to be that day.

Why it diverges from the market rate

Market exchange rates are set by currency trading — driven by interest rates, investment flows, and trade in financial assets. Purchasing power reflects local prices for goods and services, many of which (housing, haircuts, restaurant meals, groceries produced domestically) aren't traded internationally at all and don't directly factor into currency markets. The result is that a currency can be persistently "cheap" or "expensive" relative to what its purchasing power would suggest, sometimes for years at a stretch.

A familiar real-world version: the Big Mac Index

The Economist's Big Mac Index is a well-known, simplified illustration of this idea: it compares the price of a Big Mac (a roughly standardized product sold in dozens of countries) converted at the market exchange rate, as a rough proxy for whether a currency looks over- or under-valued relative to its purchasing power. It's intentionally simplified — a single product is a blunt instrument — but it captures the intuition well.

Why this matters practically

  • For travelers: a currency that looks "weak" against your own on a converter doesn't automatically mean your money goes further there — local prices matter as much as the exchange rate.
  • For cost-of-living comparisons: comparing salaries or living costs across countries using only the market exchange rate can be misleading; PPP-adjusted comparisons are more meaningful for that specific question.
  • For economists: large, sustained gaps between a currency's market rate and its PPP-implied rate are sometimes (not always) a signal that the market rate could adjust over the long run — though "long run" can mean years, and plenty of gaps never fully close.
The distinction that matters most: the live rate on our converter tells you what you'll get when you exchange money today. Purchasing power parity tells you something different — roughly what that money is actually worth in terms of what it buys. Both are useful; neither one substitutes for the other.
