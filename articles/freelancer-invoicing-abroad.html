A Freelancer's Guide to Invoicing Clients Abroad

Working with international clients is one of the best things about freelancing — and one of the few parts of the job that quietly costs money if you don't set it up deliberately. The decisions that matter most all happen before you send the first invoice.

Which currency should you invoice in?

There's no universal right answer, but a few patterns hold up well in practice:

  • Invoice in your own currency when you want predictable income and are comfortable letting the client absorb any exchange rate movement between invoice and payment.
  • Invoice in the client's currency when it makes the relationship smoother or is standard practice in their market — common in some industries and regions — but understand you're now carrying the exchange rate risk yourself between invoicing and conversion.
  • Invoice in a stable third currency (commonly USD or EUR) when working with clients in markets that have volatile local currencies — this is standard practice for freelancers serving clients in regions with high currency instability.

Understanding the risk you're taking on

If you invoice in a client's currency and the exchange rate moves against you between sending the invoice and converting the payment, your effective income drops — even though the client paid exactly what was agreed. For small, frequent invoices this rarely matters much. For large one-off projects with 30- or 60-day payment terms, the gap between invoicing and payment can be long enough for meaningful rate movement.

A practical hedge: for large invoices with long payment terms, consider requesting a deposit up front (converted and banked immediately) with the balance due on delivery, rather than one large invoice paid entirely at the end. It reduces how much value is exposed to rate movement over the full project timeline.

Fees to watch for on the receiving end

How you receive international payments matters as much as which currency you invoice in:

  • Traditional bank wire transfers often carry a receiving fee and a conversion margin that isn't always disclosed clearly upfront.
  • Freelance platforms (marketplaces with built-in payment processing) typically charge a percentage on international payments, on top of any platform service fee.
  • Dedicated multi-currency accounts built for freelancers and small businesses generally offer conversion rates closer to the mid-market rate, with a transparent, smaller fee — worth comparing against your bank's default wire process.

Keeping records straight

For tax purposes, most jurisdictions require income to be reported in your home currency, converted at the rate on the date payment was received (rules vary by country — check with a tax professional for your situation). Keeping a simple log of invoice date, payment date, amount in the original currency, and the conversion rate applied makes this far less painful at tax time than reconstructing it later.
